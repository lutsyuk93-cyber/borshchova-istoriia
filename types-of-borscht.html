Overview

Main types of borscht

A quick look at how each variation differs from the classic recipe and when to make it.

01

Classic red borscht

The base version made with meat broth, beet, cabbage, potato, and a tomato-paste sauté. The foundation for every other variation.

02

Green borscht (sorrel)

Made in spring and summer with sorrel instead of beet. The taste is tangy and fresh, often served with a boiled egg.

03

Cold borscht (beet chilled soup)

A summer version with no cooked vegetables — beet, cucumber, kefir or kvass, and herbs are served chilled. Refreshing in hot weather.

04

Meatless (Lenten) borscht

Made without meat, on a vegetable or mushroom broth. Traditionally served during fasting periods, with flavor built up through the sauté and mushrooms.

05

Bean borscht

Popular in western Ukraine. Beans add heartiness and body, and often appear alongside the meatless version.

06

Lviv-style borscht

Served with small meat-filled dumplings ("vushka") or a sausage, often clearer than the classic version.

07

Polissya-style borscht

A northern version made with mushrooms and often without tomato paste — the color and flavor are milder than in southern variants.

08

Prune borscht

Prunes add a subtle sweetness and depth of flavor, pairing well with the bone-in meat version.

Frequently asked questions

About the different types of borscht

How is green borscht different from the classic one?

Instead of beet, the tang and freshness come from sorrel, so green borscht doesn't have the signature red color.

Is cold borscht served raw or cooked?

The beet for cold borscht is pre-cooked and chilled, while the fresh vegetables and herbs are added raw right before serving.

Can meatless borscht be as rich as the meat version?

Yes — a good sauté, mushroom broth, and enough garlic at the end of cooking build plenty of depth.

Which borscht is easiest for a beginner?

Classic red borscht — it's the most common and has the most detailed step-by-step recipe on the homepage.
